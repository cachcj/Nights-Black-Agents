It is 1989, and Germany is split between East and West — led by the **Soviet Union** and the **United States**. Berlin sits directly on that fault line.

But something is changing. Things are getting better. Reforms under Mikhail Gorbachev have loosened control in Eastern Europe. Borders are shifting. Protests are growing. Governments are hesitating.

No one agrees on what happens next, least of all London, Washington, or Moscow. But word on the street is that Moscow's grip is slipping. Rumour says that things are about to get violent. A little bird's told you that the Wall is about to come down.

### The City
The division of Berlin is more than just literal — it is cultural. One city split into two systems, each with their own rhythm, population, and level of scrutiny.

West Berlin is an island of the Western bloc within East Germany — open, noisy, commercially active, and full of outsiders. East Berlin, by contrast, is the capital of the DDR — controlled, deliberate, cautious. 


#### West Berlin (Allied Sectors)
##### British Sector (Charlottenburg, Wilmersdorf, Spandau)

The British sector forms the core of what most people think of as “City West.” It includes Charlottenburg and Wilmersdorf — commercial, social, and transit hubs — along with Spandau further out.

Around Berlin Zoo (_“the Zoo”_) and the nearby Kurfürstendamm (_Ku’damm_), you’ll find cafés, cinemas, department stores, and a constant flow of people. This is one of the busiest parts of West Berlin: diplomats, journalists, students, tourists, and intelligence personnel all pass through here. It’s easy to blend in, but just as easy to be one face among many being quietly observed.

The Tiergarten sits nearby — a large central park that offers space, cover, and multiple routes in and out. It’s the sort of place where a meeting can happen in plain sight or not at all.

Spandau, further west, is quieter and more suburban. It’s historically notable for Spandau Prison, which held high-profile prisoners from the war. By 1989 it’s more of a footnote than a focal point, but the area retains a slower pace and lower profile.

For MI6, this sector is natural ground: busy, international, and forgiving of movement. It’s where you meet, plan, and disappear into the background — if you’re careful.

## 🇺🇸 American Sector (Kreuzberg, Neukölln, Schöneberg, Tempelhof, Steglitz, Zehlendorf)

The American sector is broader and more varied, stretching from dense inner districts to quieter suburban edges.

Kreuzberg and Neukölln are the most immediately distinctive. They’re working-class, heavily populated, and politically active, with large immigrant communities — particularly Turkish Berliners. These districts have a reputation for protests, counterculture, and a certain unpredictability. They’re loud, crowded, and often a little rough around the edges. For an operator, that can be useful: it’s easier to lose a tail here, but harder to control what happens next.

Schöneberg sits somewhere between administrative and residential. It includes Rathaus Schöneberg, a symbolically important site during the Cold War. The area feels more orderly, with offices, apartments, and a steady daytime rhythm.

Tempelhof is dominated by Tempelhof Airport, still a potent symbol of the Berlin Airlift. Even in 1989, it carries weight — politically and historically. The surrounding district is functional and relatively quiet.

Further out, Steglitz and Zehlendorf are more suburban and affluent. Zehlendorf in particular is low-density, green, and discreet. These areas attract less attention, but they also offer fewer places to hide if something goes wrong — strangers stand out.

For MI6, the American sector offers flexibility: from chaotic inner districts ideal for blending in, to quieter areas suited for safehouses or low-profile meetings.

---

## 🇫🇷 French Sector (Wedding, Reinickendorf)

The French sector sits to the north and tends to be less prominent in day-to-day conversation, but it remains part of the Allied structure.

Wedding is dense and working-class, with a mix of residential and industrial areas. It doesn’t have the commercial pull of Charlottenburg or the edge of Kreuzberg, but it’s lived-in and practical — the sort of place where people mind their own business.

Reinickendorf is more residential and spread out, with a quieter, almost provincial feel compared to the rest of West Berlin. It sees less international traffic and fewer obvious points of interest.

Because of this, the French sector has a lower intelligence “temperature.” There are fewer diplomats, fewer journalists, fewer obvious reasons for outsiders to be there. That can make it useful for quiet operations — or risky, if you don’t have a reason to belong.

# 🟥 East Berlin (DDR Capital)

Dividing East Berlin by “sector” doesn’t make sense in the same way — it’s not jointly administered. Instead, it’s more useful to think in terms of **function and geography**: central, inner residential, and outer districts.

---

## 🏛️ Central District (Mitte)

Mitte is the political and administrative heart of East Berlin.

It includes major landmarks such as Alexanderplatz (_“Alex”_), a major transport hub and public space, and Museum Island, a cultural centre that attracts visitors, academics, and officials. The Brandenburg Gate sits nearby, though in 1989 it is inaccessible, standing at the edge of the Wall.

This district sees a steady flow of controlled traffic: diplomats, tourists, and state personnel. It is one of the few places where a foreign presence is expected — which makes it both useful and heavily observed.

For operations, Mitte offers legitimacy but little room for error. You can be here — but you will be seen.

---

## 🏢 Inner Residential Districts (Prenzlauer Berg, Friedrichshain)

These districts are densely populated and more local in character.

Prenzlauer Berg has a reputation — quiet but important — as a place where artists, intellectuals, and subtle dissent exist. By the late 1980s, there is a growing sense of dissatisfaction, though it’s rarely expressed openly. Churches and cultural spaces sometimes act as meeting points for discussion.

Friedrichshain is more working-class and closer to the Wall. It feels more grounded, less outward-facing.

In both areas, people notice strangers more readily than in the West. Not because they are actively watching, but because unfamiliar faces stand out. Conversations are more cautious, and trust is harder to establish.

For MI6, these districts are potential recruitment ground — but require patience, care, and a convincing reason to be there.

---

## 🏭 Outer Districts (Lichtenberg, Marzahn-Hellersdorf, Treptow-Köpenick)

Further out, East Berlin becomes more functional and less visible.

These districts are characterised by large housing developments (_Plattenbau_), industrial areas, and administrative facilities. Life here is structured and routine.

Lichtenberg in particular is associated with state security presence, including facilities linked to the Stasi. Other outer districts are less politically central but still tightly controlled.

These areas are not designed for outsiders. There are fewer reasons for a foreigner to be present, and fewer places to blend in. At the same time, they can house things that are meant to be out of sight — facilities, storage, or operations that don’t belong in the centre.

For play, these districts are where things become harder to explain — and where mistakes are less forgiving.
