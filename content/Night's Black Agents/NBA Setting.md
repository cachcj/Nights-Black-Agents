It is 1989, and Germany is split between East and West — led by the **Soviet Union** and the **United States**. Berlin sits directly on that fault line.

But something is changing. Things are getting better. Reforms under Mikhail Gorbachev have loosened control in Eastern Europe. Borders are shifting. Protests are growing. Governments are hesitating.

No one agrees on what happens next, least of all London, Washington, or Moscow. But word on the street is that Moscow's grip is slipping. Rumour says that things are about to get violent. A little bird's told you that the Wall is about to come down.

### The City
The division of Berlin is more than just literal — it is cultural. One city split into two systems, each with their own rhythm, population, and level of scrutiny.

West Berlin is an island of the Western bloc within East Germany — open, noisy, commercially active, and full of outsiders. East Berlin, by contrast, is the capital of the DDR — controlled, deliberate, cautious. 


#### West Berlin (Allied Sectors)
##### British Sector (Charlottenburg, Tiergarten, Spandau, Wilmersdorf)

The British sector forms the core of what most people think of as “City West". Established, structured, conservative — not quite as bustling as the American areas, not quite as overlooked as the French.

Charlottenburg is one of the most prominent and recognisable parts of West Berlin. Home to the Berlin Zoological Garden and the nearby Kurfürstendamm, you'll find the area littered with cafés, cinemas, department stores, with a constant flow of diplomats, students, and tourists you can easily melt into.

The Tiergarten sits nearby — a large central park that offers space, cover, and multiple routes in and out. It's favourite of MI6 where it comes to dead drops and clandestine meets, and for good reason.

Spandau, further west, is quieter and more suburban. It’s historically notable for Spandau Prison, which held high-profile prisoners from the war. By 1989 it’s more of a footnote than a focal point, but the area retains a slower pace and lower profile.

Similarly, Wilmersdorf to the south of Charlottenburg is an area of quiet affluence, lined with residential apartments for the middle-to-upper class. A borough where professionals can work without worrying about too many questions asked.

##### American Sector (Kreuzberg and Neukölln, Schöneberg, Tempelhof, Steglitz-Zehlendorf)

The American sector is broader, busier, and more varied. There's a stronger sense of movement here: traffic, nightlife, immigrant communities, all watched over by a visible American military presence.

Kreuzberg and Neukölln are the most immediately distinctive. They’re working-class, heavily populated, and politically active, with large immigrant communities — particularly Turkish Berliners. Loud, crowded, and rough around the edges, these districts have a reputation for protests, counterculture, and a certain unpredictability.

Schöneberg sits somewhere between administrative and residential. Between the offices and apartments lie the Rathaus Schöneberg, the seat of West Berlin's government, and where Kennedy delivered his "Ich bin ein Berliner" speech.

Tempelhof is dominated by Tempelhof Airport, still a potent symbol of the Berlin Airlift. Even in 1989, it carries weight — politically and historically. The surrounding district is functional and relatively quiet.

Further out, Steglitz-Zehlendorf are more suburban and affluent. Zehlendorf in particular is low-density, green, and discreet. These areas attract less attention, but they also offer fewer places to hide if something goes wrong.

##### French Sector (Wedding, Reinickendorf)

The French sector sits to the north and tends to be less prominent in day-to-day conversation, but it remains just as much a part of the Allied structure as the other sectors.

Wedding is dense and working-class, with a mix of residential and industrial areas. It doesn’t have the commercial pull of Charlottenburg or the edge of Kreuzberg, but it’s lived-in and practical where the ordinary citizens mind their own business.

Reinickendorf is more residential and spread out, with a quieter, almost provincial feel compared to the rest of West Berlin. It sees less international traffic and fewer obvious points of interest.

#### East Berlin (Capital of the DDR)

##### Central District (Mitte)

Mitte is the political and administrative heart of East Berlin.

It includes major landmarks such as Alexanderplatz (affectionally known to the locals as *“Alex”*), a major transport hub and public space, and Museum Island, a cultural centre that attracts visitors, academics, and officials. The Brandenburg Gate stands at the edge of the Wall, imposing even if inaccessible.

This district sees a steady flow of controlled traffic: diplomats, tourists, and state personnel. It is one of the few places where a foreign presence is expected — which makes it both useful and heavily observed.




##### Inner Residential Districts (Prenzlauer Berg, Friedrichshain)

Prenzlauer Berg has a reputation — quiet but important — as a place where artists, intellectuals, and subtle dissent exist. By the late 1980s, there is a growing sense of dissatisfaction, though it’s rarely expressed openly. Churches and cultural spaces sometimes act as meeting points for discussion.

Friedrichshain is more working-class and closer to the Wall. It feels more grounded, less outward-facing.

In both areas, people notice strangers more readily than in the West. Not because they are actively watching, but because unfamiliar faces stand out. Conversations are more cautious, and trust is harder to establish.


##### Outer Districts (Lichtenberg, Marzahn-Hellersdorf, Treptow-Köpenick)

Further out, East Berlin becomes more functional and less visible.

These districts are characterised by large housing developments (_Plattenbau_), industrial areas, and administrative facilities. Life here is structured and routine.

Lichtenberg in particular is associated with state security presence, including facilities linked to the Stasi. Other outer districts are less politically central but still tightly controlled.

These areas are not designed for outsiders. There are fewer reasons for a foreigner to be present, and fewer places to blend in. At the same time, they can house things that are meant to be out of sight — facilities, storage, or operations that don’t belong in the centre.

### The Players
By 1989, Berlin is one of the most intelligence-saturated cities in Western Europe, if not the world. Every major power has people on the ground, keeping their eyes and ears open. 

Intelligence gathering may have progressed in leaps in bounds over the years, but two groups of methods remain unchangingly relevant: HUMINT and SIGINT. The first focuses on human sources, plants and informants and recruited assets. The latter on communications and correspondence, listening posts and wire intercepts and surveillance photography.

##### MI6
Gone are the days when the Secret Intelligence Service is the dominant force in the world of espionage. Back then, British intelligence had networks, coordination, and brilliant people, but most of all, it had experience. Now, that experience is being slowly overshadowed by the resources other powers are pouring into their own intelligence agencies — manpower and funding.

What keeps MI6 relevant is approach. Leaning heavily on tried and true methods executed to perfection, as well as long-term relationships, cultural familiarity, and careful handling of human sources. Where others may push for rapid results, MI6 cultivates assets patiently, often through indirect contact and layered intermediaries. 

Officially, MI6 works out of the British Embassy. Unofficially, they work out of "the Zoo", a small group of flats overlooking the train station that is its namesake.

##### CIA
The Central Intelligence Agency rose to prominence after the Second World War as the United States assumed a leading role in global politics. Unlike Britain, which relied on established networks and experience, the CIA was built in an era of expansion, with significant funding and political backing, over time establishing itself as one of the most visible and influential intelligence agencies in the Western world.

Like many other American enterprises, the CIA benefits most from resources and scale. It can run more operations simultaneously, deploy more personnel, and integrate newer technical capabilities more extensively than most of its counterparts. It is no wonder then if their approach is bolder and more direct than MI6's — they can apply more pressure, move quicker, and afford to invest more heavily in operations with uncertain returns on interest.

##### KGB
The KGB emerged after the Second World War as the Soviet Union consolidated its internal and external security apparatus. A centralised form of the Soviets' earlier intelligence organisations, the KGB combines foreign intelligence, counterintelligence, and internal security roles. By the Cold War, it was one of the most powerful intelligence services in the world.

In East Germany, the KGB operates more as Moscow's strategic overseer. To the average citizen, it is less visible than the local security services, but its presence is understood — quiet, authoritative, oppressive. The KGB’s strengths lie in discipline, coordination, and long-term planning, perfectly suited for their goals of preserving Soviet influence.

##### Stasi
The Stasi (Ministerium für Staatssicherheit) is the most immediate and pervasive intelligence presence in East Berlin. Established to secure the East German state, it developed into one of the most extensive surveillance organisations in history. By 1989, its network includes not only full-time officers but also a vast number of informants drawn from ordinary citizens.

For the average person, the Stasi is not always visible, but it is always present. People assume that conversations may be reported, that neighbours may be informants, and that unusual behaviour will be noticed. Experts at collecting information on individuals, tracking relationships, and identifying patterns of behaviour, the Stasi's one weaknesses is the increasing turbulence in East Berlin, where events happen and opinion changes faster than they could track it.
